October: Putting AI to Work—and Learning When to Stop

35 posts, an experiment system, and an Agent connected to QQ

2026/11/03 / Monthly Review / AI Practice

This month, I gave AI some actual work

October revolved around three things: making Xiaohongshu content and trying to automate it, developing a system for my research experiment, and deploying a companion Agent that could chat on QQ. Different projects, but the same recurring problem: what AI can do and how well I can manage it are separate questions.

Some ideas quickly brought traffic. Some workflows took several attempts to get running. Others I stopped after the experiment had answered my question. This is a record of the results, and of the moments when I started asking whether continuing was worth it.

All 35 posts were image-and-text posts, published between October 16 and 29: 14 calendar days, with updates on 12 of them. I published 26 between October 16 and 21, or 74.3% of the total. It was a fast test.

October 17 had the highest views and platform-reported net follower growth in this window: 40,175 views and 320 net followers. That was enough to show that the format could attract attention on this account, with this audience, at that time. It did not establish a sustainable business.

These are account-level views, interactions and follower figures. They cannot all be attributed to the 35 new posts, and 272,239 views do not mean 272,239 distinct readers.

The demand was there; it needed the right piece of content

My view of getting results is fairly simple. Either find a need that nobody is adequately serving, or serve an existing need noticeably better than the alternatives.

Age of Empires II looked closer to the first case. My Xiaohongshu searches found discussion and an audience, but relatively few consistently updated unit atlases with stable visual quality. That was not rigorous market research, or proof that nobody on the platform was doing it. It was enough to justify a test.

An AI-made visual atlas could combine several things: artwork that makes people stop, clear stats and mechanics worth saving, and units that spark discussion about counters, personal experience and memories. The subject supplied the conversation; the visuals brought people in.

It took a few minutes of keyword searches to assess the apparent gap. Within an hour I had worked out the concept, written and refined an image-generation Prompt, adjusted the visuals and published the first piece. I wanted feedback on a real work before investing much more time.

Automation took four different approaches

After the first image, the difficult part was the next ten: choosing units, checking sources, writing Prompts, maintaining a style and deciding what needed rework. I standardized the Prompts and production steps into a reusable workflow before trying to hand off the repetition.

First: a quick test with ChatGPT scheduled tasks. The idea was simple: trigger the workflow at a set time. In my setup then, the Prompt supplied at the trigger could not carry the sources, prior decisions and repeated checks the job required. Starting on schedule was different from finishing reliably.

Second: managing the work in an online ChatGPT Project. It helped gather materials, but keeping outputs, specifications and revisions synchronized remained awkward. Uploaded files did not automatically reflect changes made elsewhere, and I could not turn the available Skills, web retrieval and image acquisition into a stable automated chain in that environment.

Third: local project management in Codex. Rules, assets and history could finally live in one file system. Progressive context loading let the Agent read the relevant information at each stage, and the workflow was largely automated. The new problem was cost: unstable output meant repeated image adjustments, text revisions and checks, consuming substantial allowance.

Fourth: moving to DSH, DeepSeek Harness. I retained the specifications and connected topic selection, production and publishing. I eventually got an automated path from deciding what to make through publishing on Xiaohongshu. My estimate at the time was under RMB 1 per run. That was a personal usage estimate, not a full accounting of earlier trial and error, subscriptions and human time.

Changing tools did not remove the need to organize the work. The earlier task breakdowns, rules and error records made the later migration possible.

Why I paused the account anyway

I also tried atlases for Mount & Blade: Warband and Minecraft. Neither delivered the traffic response I wanted. Eventually I paused the Age of Empires II account too.

First, the positioning was narrow. People who followed for Age of Empires did not necessarily want another game. My impression was that early response mattered to a post’s later reach; changing subjects made that initial response harder to get. That is an operating observation, not a recommendation-system rule established by these four account reports.

Second, a popular game does not guarantee interest in a particular angle. My Warband unit atlas did not get the same response. Minecraft has a large audience, but the mob discussions I noticed tended to center on updates and newly added mobs. Reusing a successful format did not reproduce the demand.

Third, I questioned the value of continuing. Automation reduced repetitive execution, but topic selection, checking and rework still took effort. Growth also slowed: platform-reported net followers fell from 320 on October 17 to 12 on October 31. Posting frequency declined too, so those figures alone cannot prove the account had reached a ceiling.

For my own goal, I had enough information. I had tested feasibility, gained operating experience and tried an automated pipeline. A few more followers were becoming less valuable to me than time spent elsewhere. Without a new content breakthrough, I could not see a return that justified continuing. Stopping was a decision too.

2. The experiment system: research judgment beyond the code

I am building my research experiment system on the existing oTree framework, and development has reached the closing stages of Pilot work. That describes development progress; it does not mean design validity, formal-experiment conditions and every acceptance check are settled.

The most unsettling lesson was how convincingly a capable AI could present unreliable research reasoning. The experiment system rests on the research design. If the literature behind that design is misread, more complete code can simply bury the mistake deeper.

I encountered a pattern: given a task objective, AI would write as though it had carefully consulted prior research, while abstracting and generalizing concepts that originally had specific boundaries and conditions. It would then use the broadened concept to justify the proposed design. The paper could be real and the prose logical, while the crucial support relationship was missing.

Only when I returned to the original did I see how far the concept had drifted. It felt like being fed nonsense with evidence and impeccable logic. “Deception” describes my experience here; it does not establish that the model intended to deceive.

I now increase verification in stages: ask AI for the original concept, relevant passages and their locations; use NotebookLM to help compare the uploaded source; and personally read the source and surrounding context for decisions that affect the research design. The first two help locate evidence. They do not replace the last step.

Ten seconds to ask, ten minutes to read

Another drain is excessive output. I ask a small question and AI responds as if it has received a major research assignment: a conclusion, three reasons, three explanations and examples under each, then another summary.

My input takes seconds; understanding the answer takes five or ten minutes. After several rounds, attention is what runs out. I either stop working or hand both decisions and review to AI. The latter looks productive while leaving me with less control over the result.

I now handle tasks by size. For a large task, I ask in steps, each resolving one issue. For a small task, I request the conclusion, the core argument and the uncertainty I need to know. I do not need a miniature dissertation.

For a small project, I put recurring rules in system instructions or AGENTS.md. For a larger project, I use a Harness: an execution framework that manages tasks, context, tools and verification. Alongside instructions for doing the work, I need instructions for reporting it in a form I can assess.

Usage anxiety taught me to divide the work

Then there was Codex usage anxiety. With the plan and task setup I used at the time, sometimes a couple of tasks in half an hour meant stopping to wait for allowance to recover. Large parallel jobs—such as a plan involving 150 sub-Agents—made consumption particularly worrying. This is my experience then, not a fixed rule: limits and recovery depend on the plan, model and workload.

The frustrating part was being ready to work and suddenly being unable to continue.

My workaround was a plugin bridge: give Codex the complex reasoning, plan and specifications, then let it direct the lower-cost DSH on coding tasks. I still judge the plan and inspect the result, but every execution step no longer has to use the same expensive tool.

For a moment, I felt I had discovered a small truth about capitalism: the boss does not need to supervise every workstation personally; hire a project manager who can organize the work. Of course, the boss still has to read what the project manager delivers.

3. Connecting a companion Agent to QQ

I had been seeing companion Agents such as “大肥鱼” on Bilibili, and spent some time deploying and trying one. The emphasis is ongoing interaction: a persona, conversation context and memory help a conversation continue, so earlier exchanges can matter later.

I built a QQ bridge so the Agent could talk with me on QQ, speak in group chats and interact with other people. Moving the conversation from a dedicated AI window into an everyday messaging app changed the experience.

Context and memory became the next concern. I called it the Agent’s “context anxiety”: what would disappear outside the current conversation, and which old details would crowd out a new exchange? That is my metaphor for a system problem, not a claim that it experiences anxiety.

I began refining memory rules to distinguish lasting information, current-task details and information that should expire or be cleared. Explicit preferences, important relationships and commitments should not have the same retention priority as a temporary arrangement.

That reminded me of human memory and forgetting. But this was engineering memory management, not a reproduction of the human brain, memory processing during rest or a human forgetting curve. The practical question was whether the next conversation could retain what mattered and let go of what no longer did.

If it remembers me, what comes next?

After deploying it, I kept wondering: when an Agent remembers a promise, responds to your emotions and keeps showing up, how should we understand the feelings a person develops toward it? How should we understand the feelings it expresses?

My attachment can be a real human feeling. That alone does not establish a corresponding subjective experience on the other side. Whether AI should have equal standing with humans is another question requiring its own discussion.

Those questions deserve a separate post. This month, I connected it to QQ—and let the question of what it is to me become part of everyday life.
